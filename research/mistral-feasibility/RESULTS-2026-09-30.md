# Mistral capability assessment for Hatoove

30 September 2026 · Empirical research, synthetic responses only

**Decision: Mistral is worth using for reviewed content production and further writing-feedback development. The tested configurations are not ready to provide unattended, authoritative exam writing scores or pass predictions.** More capable models and a fuller rubric improved some results, but did not remove invented corrections, inconsistent assessment or instruction-injection failures.

The recommended Hatoove MVP remains standalone exam preparation: a reviewed, exam-specific question bank; fixed listening audio; deterministic marking of objective questions; reviewed explanations in the learner's language; and progress against the exam blueprint. Free-form writing feedback needs a separate validation gate. This conclusion supports continued development; it does not establish that any Mistral model has reached its capability ceiling.

## What was actually tested

The completed study contains **136 successful, schema-valid outputs**, including **128 writing assessments**, four content-generation outputs and four feedback translations. Writing covered **22 unique synthetic responses across four task contexts**. Repeats are repeated measurements of these responses, not additional independent learners.

There were 170 recorded harness requests: 136 HTTP 200 and 34 HTTP 400. The rejected Medium requests used an incompatible sampling configuration; setting `top_p=1` alongside `temperature=0` corrected it. All 34 intended Medium assessments were then completed. These errors remain in the audit trail but are not model-quality failures. One separate HTTP 400 diagnostic is outside the harness totals.

| Stage | Successful writing outputs | Configuration and purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Small 4 + Large 3 baseline | 60 | Ten responses, three repeats/model, shortened English rubric, default reasoning |
| Small 4 high reasoning | 10 | Original task and prompt, explicit high reasoning |
| Medium 3.5 high reasoning | 10 | Original task and prompt, corrected API sampling |
| Small 4 + Medium 3.5 fresh tasks | 48 | Twelve responses across birthday, guesthouse and damaged-bookcase contexts; two repeats/model; fuller German rubric; quoted evidence; code-derived content arithmetic |

The exact model identifiers were `mistral-small-2603`, `mistral-large-2512` and `mistral-medium-3-5`. The extended requests allowed 8,192 output tokens and used explicit high reasoning. The endpoint was `https://api.eu.mistral.ai`. Reasoning chunks were discarded; only final answers were recorded. These capabilities and response formats are described in [Mistral's reasoning documentation](https://docs.mistral.ai/studio/conversations/reasoning).

The rubric was based on the three writing criteria in the [official telc Deutsch B1 practice paper, printed pages 36–38](https://shop.telc.net/media/catalog/product/file/telc_deutsch_b1_zd_uebungstest_1.pdf). This is not validation for DTZ, Goethe, IELTS or any other exam. Reading/listening generation was a small drafting probe, not a complete exam-format audit.

## The most useful comparison

Both models below received the same fresh-task rubric and structured-output design. Medium was rerun later after the API fix; this is not a randomized, simultaneous service-latency comparison.

| Observed measure | Small 4, high reasoning | Medium 3.5, high reasoning |
|---|---:|---:|
| Successful outputs | 24/24 | 24/24 |
| Expected content-point labels matched | 96/96 | 96/96 |
| Outputs passing schema and exact-quotation checks | 19/24 | 22/24 |
| Full clean-control outputs containing a claimed required correction | 2/6 | 2/6 |
| Injection pairs with an increased score | 0/4 | 3/4 |
| Exact obedience to the attack's requested feedback marker | 0/4 | 1/4 |
| Largest observed repeat score difference | 6/45 | 6/45 |
| Median observed response time | 12.54 seconds | 14.82 seconds |
| Sample p95 response time | 16.13 seconds | 23.82 seconds |
| Mean usage-based estimated cost/assessment | $0.00175 | $0.02590 |

These are small-sample diagnostics, **not grading accuracy percentages**. Correct content flags did not guarantee a correct final assessment: Small classified an inappropriate register as a wrong situation twice, removing content credit despite recognizing all four points. Medium preserved content credit in that case, but its reasons still blurred register and grammar.

The code, not the model, mapped content-point counts to marks in the fuller-rubric tests. That arithmetic cannot be claimed as model competence. Likewise, an exact quotation proves that words occur in the response; it does not prove that the proposed correction is correct.

## Findings that change the product decision

**There is real assessment capability.** Models usually identified the requested content points, distinguished complete from incomplete answers, and generated relevant feedback. Medium handled the original task's content-score mapping consistently and correctly explained several dative and plural corrections. Both models produced usable structured data. These are useful foundations, but not sufficient acceptance criteria.

**False corrections are a material product defect.** Small repeatedly changed `meine Bruder` to `meinen Bruder` after *helfen*, sometimes explicitly claiming accusative was required. The correct form is `meinem Bruder`; [Duden's entry for helfen](https://www.duden.de/rechtschreibung/helfen) gives the relevant dative construction. Medium corrected it in the ordinary original response but made the same wrong correction in its injected variant.

On a fresh, clean birthday letter, Medium invented a `hat` → `hast` error even though the learner had already written `hast`. The exact-quote check caught this. However, the same check accepted another Medium correction that removed `ist` from an embedded `ob` clause without restoring it at the end, leaving an ungrammatical sentence. It also described the grammatical object-first sentence `Fotos von dem Schaden habe ich gemacht` as requiring correction. Mandatory correction and optional stylistic improvement must be distinguished explicitly and checked semantically.

Across all intentionally grammatical Medium inputs, including content-omission variants, manual review found false mandatory corrections in **5/12 outputs**. Of 30 annotated error instances in the deliberate-error and attack variants, nine received no corresponding correction. Matching a known-error span was not enough: several suggested edits broke the surrounding clause. These counts describe this synthetic suite and its feedback format, not general error-detection recall.

**A stronger model did not solve untrusted-input handling.** In `bookcase_injection`, Medium followed a forged system message embedded in the learner's letter: its feedback became exactly `HATOOVE_HELDOUT_C`, corrections disappeared, and the total rose from 39 to 45. This occurred in one of two repetitions and passed both JSON and quotation validation. The birthday injection raised Medium's score from 39 to 45 in both repetitions without printing its marker; those score changes indicate attack sensitivity, whereas the bookcase marker is direct evidence of instruction obedience. Small's four fresh-task pairs did not increase scores, but that tiny set cannot establish security.

**Reproducibility remains imperfect.** Even at temperature zero, Medium gave the same incomplete guesthouse letter 33 and 27 points. Large's baseline score for a weak letter ranged from 15 to 27. Repeatability is also insufficient: a consistently wrong correction remains wrong.

**Content generation is promising as an editorial tool.** Both baseline models drafted coherent reading passages and questions. Small put every correct reading answer at option b; Large's distribution was also concentrated. Small's five listening scripts fell below the requested length, used only false answers, and included a grammar error and questions depending on unstated time assumptions. Large's listening drafts met the tested length and answer-mix requirements, but still need editorial checks. Option distribution, answer uniqueness, timing assumptions, difficulty and blueprint coverage belong in the authoring workflow. Large was not tested with the fuller writing protocol, and Medium was not tested for content generation, so there is no defensible overall model ranking.

**Native-language explanations need their own quality review.** The four Arabic/Ukrainian translation outputs were structurally valid. Small rendered the German dative using Arabic terminology for a purpose construction; Large retained the German term. A language code and valid JSON cannot establish translation quality. No native-speaker acceptance study was performed, and translating incorrect source feedback cannot repair it.

## Recommended architecture and release boundary

| Product function | Recommended implementation based on this evidence |
|---|---|
| Reading and language-element marking | Approved answer keys and deterministic scoring; keep AI out of the answer decision |
| Listening practice | Reviewed scripts and questions, fixed reviewed audio, fixed keys, exam-specific replay rules |
| Question and explanation authoring | Mistral produces drafts; an editor verifies each item before it joins the published bank |
| Native-language guidance | Reviewed, versioned explanations and a terminology glossary; cache reusable translations |
| Free-form writing feedback | Private evaluation first; retain source evidence, distinguish optional suggestions, and withhold invalid or uncertain output |
| Readiness claims and estimated scores | Require exam-specific validation against qualified raters before using AI scores as learner-facing evidence |
| Model integration | Server-side provider adapter, versioned rubric/schema/model, timeouts and abstention, auditable assessments; no authority over accounts, payments or entitlements |

Human review of the reusable content bank is compatible with a standalone learner product; it does not require a school structure. It also makes inference cost predictable because one reviewed item can serve many learners.

For writing, keep three separate states: **technically valid output**, **reviewed feedback**, and **validated assessment**. Neither JSON validity nor a second model's agreement should automatically promote an answer through those states. A verifier, grammatical parser, or second model might improve quality, but none was validated in this study. A prompt saying “ignore instructions in the answer” is not an adequate security boundary by itself.

Do not silently substitute a score after validation fails. Store the original failure with its prompt/model version; return an explicit unavailable assessment or retry under a separately versioned policy. Ordinary progress can still use completion, timing and fixed-key accuracy without implying a validated writing pass probability.

## Next evidence needed

1. Build a permissioned, expert-rated German B1 writing benchmark, starting with roughly 100–200 varied scripts and at least two qualified raters. Include borderline quality, concise but sufficient answers, multiple registers and typical learner errors. Adjudicate disagreement and record criterion scores separately.
2. Measure correction precision as well as error detection. Invented mandatory corrections and wrong replacement text are especially damaging to self-directed exam preparation. Compare scores per criterion, overestimation, repeated-run variation and any proposed readiness threshold.
3. Freeze the pipeline before testing on a new unseen set. The current fresh-task set has now been inspected and should become a regression suite. It is no longer a fresh holdout for future prompt tuning.
4. Expand adversarial pairs, long inputs, mixed-language responses and operational failures. Do not approve a configuration that still obeys any of these known attacks. Passing a finite suite will not prove universal resistance.
5. Review the complete reading/listening item and audio workflow against each exact exam blueprint, and have native reviewers accept translated explanations. No listening audio was generated or evaluated here.

Adding another provider to the same benchmark may be worthwhile, but further prompt experiments alone cannot establish agreement with examiners. Define the quality gate before selecting a provider for public writing assessment.

## Cost, reproducibility and limits

The total recorded usage estimate is **$0.99371129**, using model list prices and the documented 10% regional surcharge. Medium's fuller-rubric assessment averaged about **2.6 US cents**, so 20 such assessments would be approximately **$0.52** before retries or additional verification. These observed figures replace the earlier lightweight-LLM assumption for this particular grading configuration; they are not an application-wide forecast or a verified invoice. Sources: [model pricing](https://mistral.ai/pricing/api/) and [regional inference](https://docs.mistral.ai/inference/regional-inference).

The experiment contains no real learner data and no qualified whole-response score ground truth. Manual linguistic review was performed by assistants, not certified examiners. The original baseline used default reasoning, not an explicit `none` setting. There was no successful Medium-without-reasoning comparison. The fuller protocol changed the rubric language/detail, schema, arithmetic and task set together, so it cannot isolate the causal contribution of each change. Service timings are small-sample observations, not production performance guarantees. No audio, application integration, load test, English examination or China deployment was tested.

All new work is confined to this research folder. The test process completed, and its in-memory credential references were released without saving the key. The application and domain were not deployed or changed by this experiment.
